How a turntable plays a record

The short version: A diamond the size of a grain of sand rides a wiggling groove, and a magnet and a coil turn that wiggle into a tiny signal that a phono preamp lifts and corrects. Most skips, hiss, lopsided stereo and worn records come down to a few settings and storage habits. Playing a record does wear it, though the two classic lab studies disagree about what happens to the groove.

How does one groove carry two channels?#

A stereo LP has one groove with two walls, and the two recording directions sit at 90 degrees to each other and at 45 degrees to the record surface.119 Put a signal on one channel alone and only one wall is modulated; the other stays smooth.119 That is the 45/45 system, which Westrex showed to engineers in Hollywood on August 26, 1957, and which the Record Industry Association of America adopted "as the American standard" on December 27, 1957.15 A rival vertical-lateral system had backing from Sugden in the UK and Telefunken in Germany, and Decca was experimenting with it too.15 One reason 45/45 won: both channels were cut the same way, so they used the same equalization and carried the same rumble, where a vertical system would have put more rumble in one channel.15 The history covers how stereo records reached the public.

In practice the stylus does not read two walls separately. A 1958 magazine explainer called that idea a misconception, since "it is impossible for the stylus to follow different contours on both walls of the groove at the same time."120 The tip moves in a mix of sideways (lateral) and up-and-down (vertical) motion, and real music traces angled, circular or elliptical paths.120

Which phase gives which motion is where the 1958 sources part ways. The Westrex engineers wrote that in-phase signals cut a vertical groove and out-of-phase signals a lateral one.119 Norman Crowhurst wrote the opposite: in-phase gives lateral motion, out-of-phase gives vertical.120 The split comes from how each defines channel phase. The usual modern explanation, that a mono signal moves the stylus purely sideways, follows Crowhurst.

Which wall is which is settled: the inner wall carries the left channel and the outer wall the right.121 The standard itself, IEC 60098, has a clause on channel phasing, but its wording isn't in the free preview.122

The design was built to stay compatible. An ordinary lateral pickup with decent vertical give would still play back the lateral part of both channels, Westrex wrote, and each 45/45 channel came out about 3 dB below a mono lateral record with the same playing time.119

What is the difference between moving magnet and moving coil?#

Both types make a signal by moving a magnet relative to a coil. In a moving magnet (MM) cartridge the magnet rides on the cantilever, the tiny arm that carries the stylus, and the coils stay put; in a moving coil (MC) cartridge it is the other way round.123 MC designs use fewer turns or thinner wire to keep the moving parts light, which is why their output is lower.123

Moving magnet (MM) Moving coil (MC)
What moves magnet on the cantilever coils on the cantilever
Typical output124 4-8 mV, many around 4 mV usually under 2.5 mV
Example (maker claim)123 Ortofon 2M Blue, 5.5 mV Ortofon Quintet Blue, 0.5 mV
Phono gain suggested124 about 40 dB 60 dB or more

On price, What Hi-Fi? listed a Goldring E3 MM at GBP 89 / USD 189 and the Quintet Blue MC at GBP 389 / USD 569 in 2023 (reported retail), and advised staying with MM if the turntable cost under GBP 1,000 / USD 1,250.123

The two types are also serviced differently. Ortofon says the styli in its MC cartridges "are non-replaceable," so it runs an exchange scheme that buys back a worn or damaged MC at 25% of current list price against a new one of the same grade or better, while its MM cartridges are serviced by fitting a new stylus (policy as published in 2026).125 That is one maker's policy, not an industry rule.

Which stylus shape should I care about?#

The shape of the diamond decides how much of the groove wall it touches. Audio-Technica's 2025 catalogue rates its own styli by shape; the hours are the maker's figures, not a law of nature.

Elliptical more elongated, smaller contact area mid-range to high end about 300 h
Shibata very narrow, flat contact surface high end about 800 h
MicroLine narrow contact surface high end about 1,000 h

For a sense of scale, Ortofon specifies the elliptical tip on its 2M Red with radii of 8 and 18 µm.128 A 1968 RCA study used a 0.2 by 0.7 mil elliptical and a 0.7 mil conical.129

How long does a stylus last?#

It depends on whose number you read. Audio-Technica rates its elliptical at about 300 hours,127 while Ortofon says the elliptical 2M Red can reach up to 1,000 hours with proper care "without degradation of performance."128 K&B Audio puts conical at about 500 hours, crediting Audio-Technica, which is the top of A-T's own range.126 House of Marley says styli usually last about 1,000 hours.130 Go by the hours your cartridge's maker publishes, and distrust any number that claims to cover every stylus.

How much should the stylus weigh on the record?#

Tracking force is the downward weight on the stylus, set with the tonearm's counterweight. Set it to the cartridge maker's figure: too light and the record skips, too heavy and it can damage the groove.130 The Ortofon 2M Red, for example, has a range of 1.6-2.0 g with 1.8 g recommended.128 Forces used to be much heavier. The 1958 Westrex pickup tracked at 6 g,119 and in 1968 about 5 g was common on everyday players.129

There is an engineering reason light tracking mistracks. Westrex wrote that the stylus's static deflection from tracking force has to be large compared with the biggest vertical modulation in the groove, or the tip leaves the groove.119

There is no single right number across makers. Audio-Technica says the recommended force "varies from cartridge to cartridge," and its 2015 setup guide gives 2 g for its AT95E and 3.5 g for its ATP-2XN.131

What does anti-skate actually do?#

A pivoted, offset tonearm has a quirk. Friction drags the stylus along the groove, but the arm can only resist force along its own length, and the leftover pulls it toward the center of the record.132 That is skating force. Louder passages and a dirty stylus or record mean more friction and more skating, and a 12-inch arm skates less than a shorter one.132 Left alone, the stylus leans on the inner groove wall, which can cause mistracking and uneven wear on both stylus and record.132 Anti-skate is an outward bias that cancels it.

How much is in dispute. Kogen measured skating at about 20% of tracking force for an elliptical and 15-18% for a conical in 1967; Boisclair estimated about 10%; Stereophile suggests starting near 10% and adjusting by ear.132 Then the advice conflicts. House of Marley says to set the anti-skate dial to match the tracking force, and to back it off if the stylus skips outward.130 Stereophile calls anti-skate equal to tracking force "way too high."132 Both can be right, because the dial is not a force gauge: Korf measured actual anti-skate at roughly 5-30% of the number on the dial.132 The Library of Congress says to check tracking and anti-skate at least monthly, or whenever mistracking shows up.133

Do I need a phono preamp?#

Yes, built in or separate. A cartridge's signal is far below line level, and the phono preamp (or phono stage) does two jobs: it adds gain, and it applies the inverse RIAA curve.124 A PHONO/LINE switch on a turntable turns its built-in stage on or off.124

The RIAA curve exists because of cutting. The lathe boosts treble and cuts bass so the groove's swing stays more even across the range, and playback does the reverse to get back to flat.57 The curve is defined by three time constants, 3180, 318 and 75 µs, with corners near 50 Hz, 500 Hz and 2,122 Hz.57 A true constant-amplitude cut would need about 60 dB more gain at 20 kHz than at 20 Hz; RIAA settles for about 40 dB.57 IEC 98 was first published in 1964, and a 1976 amendment added a 7950 µs infrasonic roll-off, -3.0 dB at 20 Hz, which drew criticism.57 Nearly all vinyl follows RIAA, apart from some pre-1954 records.124

The RIAA Technical Committee approved the curve on January 29, 1954, and the document circulated that June. The original 1954 text has not turned up.13

Does direct drive or belt drive matter?#

In direct drive the platter sits on the motor; belt drive turns it through a belt; idler drive uses a rubber wheel.134 For a listener at home, makers sell good decks of every type. For a DJ, direct drive changed everything: high torque, near-instant start, steady speed and a platter you can push around or spin backward without harm.135 Records and DJs picks up from there.

The first direct-drive deck was the Technics SP-10, designed by Shuichi Obata. IEEE Spectrum dates it to 1969;135 Technics says it launched in 1970 as "the world's first direct drive turntable."136

What do wow, flutter and rumble mean on a spec sheet?#

Wow is slow pitch wobble and flutter is fast pitch wobble; both are given as a percentage, and lower is better.134 Rumble is noise from the deck's own moving parts that the stylus picks up, and makers often leave it off.134 As a reference point, Panasonic lists the SL-1200MK7 at 0.025% wow and flutter (weighted RMS), 0.7 seconds to reach 33 1/3 rpm and 0.18 N·m of starting torque, and gives no rumble figure.137

How should I clean records?#

Start with hands. The Library of Congress says to handle discs by the edge and label, with clean, dry hands, and to let discs from cool storage reach room temperature before playing.138 It also says to brush the stylus with a dense bristle brush before each side.133

Then the records. People argue about the best method more than about what each one does:

Method What it does
Sink wash, mild detergent common; Pspatial Audio suggests lukewarm mild detergent, a distilled-water rinse and air-drying, keeping the label dry, and says there seems to be "no systematic evidence" against dish soap139
Brush bath (Spin-Clean type) removes dust, softens fingerprints and mold140
Vacuum machine removes large and small dirt, ideally with a final pure-water rinse140
Ultrasonic the only method AnalogPlanet's author says "effectively removes in-groove contaminants"; 40 kHz for larger dirt, 120 kHz for in-groove dirt, and below about 30 kHz cavitation may damage PVC140
Wood-glue peel no serious evidence, and the glue is likely acidic, about pH 3139

Nobody has published a microscope study of ultrasonic cleaning on records, even though some archives endorse it.139

Why does static matter?#

Fresh PVC records come out of the mold with a strong negative charge, which pulls in dust.139 A 1980 microscope study in Audio rated static a minor cause of crackle next to groove fracture; its main harm is attracting grit that scours the groove.141 Remedies in the literature include carbon-fiber brushes, conductive mats, polonium-210 neutralizer bars and anti-static compounds; detergent sprays give only "very temporary relief."139 The Library of Congress recommends high-density polyethylene inner sleeves.138

How should I store records?#

Upright, on edge, with immovable dividers every 4-6 inches, and without mixing sizes.138 Records are heavy: the Library of Congress puts grooved discs at more than 35 pounds per shelf-foot.138 At home it suggests room temperature or below and about 35-40% relative humidity, away from attics and basements; for permanent storage, 46-50°F and 30-40% RH.138 The Library's own advice has shifted: its older 2002 "Nutshell" sheet gave 45-50% RH for long-term storage.133

What warps a record?#

Heat. It relaxes the stresses molded into the PVC, and a hot car is the classic case. Keep records below 70°F (21°C) and out of light; flattening a warp with heat and pressure is "never certain."139 At about 140°F (60°C) warping is all but guaranteed, and in a 2021 Discogs survey, known only secondhand, 22% of collectors named warps as their most common damage.142 Short dividers and mixed diameters on a shelf may contribute to warping too.133

Why does my record keep skipping?#

A skip is the stylus losing the groove. The usual causes: a deck that is not level, tracking force too light, anti-skate wrong (skipping outward means too much), a dirty or worn stylus, a warp, worn grooves (white streaks, skips in the same spot), and dust.130 A record weight can flatten a warp but is not a permanent fix.130 Sometimes the record arrived that way: a 1980 microscope study found small holes and surface flaws even on an unplayed audiophile pressing.141

Does playing a record wear it out?#

Yes, and the two best-known studies disagree about how. In RCA's 1968 electron-microscope study by Woodward and colleagues, a 0.2 by 0.7 mil elliptical at 1.5 g left visible "plastic-flow of the groove wall" after one play, building over 10 and 50 plays; a 0.7 mil conical at 5 g nearly wiped out 20 kHz detail after 50 plays; the same conical at 1.5 g showed essentially no wear after 50.129 The study put a critical tracking weight somewhere between 1.5 g and 5 g, and its authors still preferred the elliptical, since the conical's first-play distortion was more than twice as high.129

In 1980, Walter E. Davies played records at 1 g with anti-skate set, at an estimated 30,000-69,000 psi of contact pressure, and found mostly small chip-like fractures rather than large permanent deformation.141 After 50 plays one record was "almost unlistenable," critical listeners heard loss after 2-10 plays, and the vinyl's formulation strongly affected wear.141 So Woodward saw flow and Davies saw fracture, in different decades with different vinyl and pickups. Where they meet is practical: lighter, correctly set tracking and a clean stylus and record go easier on the groove.

Quick answers

How long does a turntable stylus last?
It depends on the maker and shape. Audio-Technica's 2025 catalogue rates its conical styli at about 300-500 hours, elliptical at about 300 and MicroLine at about 1,000, while Ortofon says its elliptical 2M Red can reach up to 1,000 hours.
What's the difference between moving magnet and moving coil cartridges?
A moving magnet cartridge has the magnet on the cantilever and fixed coils; a moving coil cartridge reverses that. MC output is lower, usually under 2.5 mV against 4-8 mV for MM, so MC needs more phono gain.
Do I need a phono preamp for my turntable?
Yes, either built into the turntable or separate. It lifts the cartridge's tiny signal to line level and applies the inverse RIAA curve; a PHONO/LINE switch on a turntable selects a built-in stage.
Why does my record keep skipping?
The stylus is losing the groove. Common causes are a deck that is not level, tracking force too light, wrong anti-skate, a dirty or worn stylus, a warp, worn grooves or dust.
Does playing vinyl wear it out?
Yes, to a degree. A 1968 RCA study found wear rose sharply between 1.5 g and 5 g of tracking force, and a 1980 study found chip-like fractures that grew with plays; the two disagree on the mechanism.
How should I store vinyl records?
Upright on edge with full-height dividers every 4-6 inches, without mixing sizes. The Library of Congress suggests room temperature or below and about 35-40% relative humidity at home, away from attics and basements.
