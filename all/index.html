The whole album

A field guide to the vinyl record: how the sound gets cut into a lacquer, plated into metal and pressed into plastic, what all those sizes, speeds and colors change, what the scratched codes in the dead wax mean, how a turntable reads a groove, how DJs and collectors use records, what one costs and who gets paid, and where in the world they are made. Written for anyone, anywhere.

A record is a sound you can see. Hold one to the light and the loud passages look wider than the quiet ones, the gaps between songs are smooth bands, and the last half inch before the label carries handwriting: a catalog number, a letter, sometimes a joke, scratched there by the person who cut it.

It is also a format that should not have survived. In the US in 2025, vinyl brought in $1,042.9 million on 46.8 million units, against 29.5 million CDs - the 19th year of vinyl growth in a row.24 The UK bought 7.6 million LPs in 2025, its 18th straight rise.29

The route runs from the lathe that cuts the master, through the father, mother and stamper, to the steam press and the paper sleeve; then on to the needle, the DJ booth, the collector's grading sheet and the bill. It starts with a short history, and there is a decoder for the runout groove: type in what is scratched in your own records and find out who made them.

Every factual claim is footnoted to the manufacturer, standard, filing, archive or reporting it came from, and every price and sales figure carries its year. Sentences without a footnote are explanation. What couldn't be pinned down sits in the open questions at the end instead of being guessed at. The Groove is independent and not affiliated with any label, plant, store or brand named in it; cutting engineers, plant crews and collectors are welcome at the corrections desk.

Track 1

A short history of the record

The short version: The record went from tinfoil cylinders in 1877 to shellac 78s to Columbia's vinyl LP in 1948 and RCA's 45 in 1949, which split the market into albums and singles. Cassettes and then CDs pushed vinyl to a rounding error by the mid-2000s. Since then US vinyl has grown 19 years in a row, passing $1 billion in revenue in 2025.

Who made the first record?#

Thomas Edison developed the phonograph in 1877 while working on telegraph and telephone equipment; the first machine recorded onto a metal cylinder wrapped in tinfoil, which wore out after a few playings.1 A diaphragm moved by the voice made a stylus vibrate up and down, pressing a track into the foil. Edison got there by running the phonautograph backward - a device about 20 years older that could trace sound but not replay it - and the Smithsonian calls his machine "the first instrument ever to record and play back sound."2

The often-cited August 12, 1877, is probably too early. The patent application wasn't filed until December 24, 1877, and historians think the first working recording came later than the August date.1 The Edison Speaking Phonograph Company was set up on January 24, 1878, to exhibit the machine, and the patent issued on February 19, 1878.1 Paid demonstrations followed that spring and summer, and in April 1878 Edison licensed it as an office dictation machine. Sales were poor, production stopped, and Edison did not come back to it until 1888, with 17 new patent applications.2

In the gap, others improved it. C. Bell and Tainter swapped tinfoil for wax, patenting their graphophone on May 4, 1886. Edison adopted wax cylinders too, forming the Edison Phonograph Company on October 8, 1887, and selling an "Improved Phonograph" by May 1888.1

The first words on that first 1877 recording were the opening of "Mary Had a Little Lamb."3

Did the cylinder or the disc win?#

The disc won, and Edison eventually said so with his product line. Emile Berliner received his gramophone patent on November 8, 1887, moving from cylinders to "flat, double-sided zinc discs with spiral grooves." The case for the flat disc was industrial: it could be mass-produced far more cheaply than a cylinder.4

Berliner's early discs were a materials lab. There were experimental zinc discs and very rare celluloid and rubber ones, but most surviving Berliners were pressed from a shellac compound by two outside firms, the Duranoid Company and the Burt Company. The earliest known list of Berliner records is a scrapbook page dated November 1, 1894, and production stopped in 1900.5 That year a court injunction shut the Berliner Gramophone Company of Philadelphia; Berliner passed his patent rights to Eldridge Johnson, who formed the Victor Talking Machine Company, with Berliner keeping a financial interest.4

Edison announced the Edison Disc Phonograph in 1913, conceding that the public preferred discs. He kept making Blue Amberol cylinders anyway, right up to the company's end in 1929.1

What were 78s made of, and why do they shatter?#

A shellac 78 is mostly rock dust held together by bug resin. The shellac compound was the resin plus fillers, typically clay, slate or limestone, with carbon black for color and cotton fibers for strength.7 Shellac 78s were made commercially from 1897 until the late 1950s, with use falling off steadily after World War II.7

The name 78 promises more consistency than the era delivered. Speeds ran 30, 70 or 78 rpm, and common sizes were 7, 10, 12 and 16 inches.7 The Internet Archive's Great 78 Project puts it flatly: "During the 78rpm era there were no standards for speed, stylus size, or record/playback equalization." Its transfer engineer plays every disc at 78.26 rpm, a long-standing trade convention.8 Pre-1948 discs mostly have wide coarse grooves; the microgroove records that followed carry nearly twice as many.7

How long 78s hung on depended on the country. Japanese 78 specialists, passed along on an archivists' mailing list, put the last new-issue 78s in Japan in 1958 and the last reissue pressings in 1962 - secondhand dates nobody has documented.9

The last US-issued 78 is usually named as Chuck Berry's "Too Pooped to Pop" on Chess, released in February 1960, though small labels almost certainly put out later ones.10 EMI deleted 78s from its catalogs in 1962, on the word of a single source, and by about 1960 78 sales were "virtually gone in the US and western Europe."10

When did the LP come out?#

In June 1948, at a press event at the Waldorf Astoria in New York, Columbia unveiled the long-playing record. The Library of Congress dates the announcement June 21; a 1948 Billboard item, cited by a reader on the Library's own post, and Matsubayashi Kohji's microgroove.jp both say June 18.11

The engineering came first. Peter Goldmark led the Columbia team that perfected the microgroove in the summer of 1947. The first LP catalog number, ML 4001, was the Mendelssohn Violin Concerto with Nathan Milstein and the New York Philharmonic under Bruno Walter. Early sides held about 23 minutes; Columbia's 1949 catalog claimed "up to 45 minutes of music on one 12-inch record," made of "nonbreakable Vinylite."11 Columbia's president, Edward Wallerstein, had been here before: he was the one who halted RCA Victor's failed long-playing disc of the early 1930s.11

The material mattered as much as the groove. Plastic discs, made of polyvinyl chloride or polystyrene with stabilizers, arrived in the late 1940s and are still what gets pressed.7

Why are records 33 1/3 and 45?#

Because two companies fought for two years and both won. On January 9, 1949, Columbia announced a 7-inch single at 33 1/3. The next day RCA Victor introduced its own 7-inch at 45 rpm, code-named "Madame X," with a dedicated changer; the big center hole allowed a smaller changing mechanism.12 RCA also claimed the 45 used less vinyl compound per pressing.12

This was the war of the speeds, and the rest of the industry had to pick a side. In August 1949 Decca went with the LP and Capitol went with all three speeds. In April 1950 RCA began issuing LPs, and in August 1950 Columbia began issuing 45s.12 Nobody won outright: the LP became the album, the 45 became the single.12 Today's sizes and speeds are in formats.

What is the RIAA curve, and why does it still matter?#

Every record is cut with the bass turned down and the treble turned up, and every phono preamp undoes it. That deliberate tilt is an equalization curve: cutting less bass keeps the groove from swinging too wide, and boosting treble lifts it above surface noise; playback reverses both. Before 1954 every label could pick its own. Columbia's LP used a curve close to the 1942 NAB standard, RCA's 45 used its "Old Orthophonic" curve, and the mess built support for one standard.12

The RIAA curve was approved by the RIAA Technical Committee, with chief engineers of five major US labels, on January 29, 1954, and circulated formally by June 1954.13 Matsubayashi calls it "essentially identical" to RCA Victor's New Orthophonic curve, already in use. It kept the 75-microsecond high-frequency setting from the June 1953 NARTB standard, which had replaced NAB's 100 microseconds of 1942.13

Labels didn't all switch at once. Radio News reported in July 1954 that RCA Victor, Columbia, Capitol (already using it), London (recutting its back catalog), Decca and MGM (future releases) and Mercury were on board, while Westminster stayed with NAB for the time being.13 A record cut before its label switched may not match the curve a modern phono stage assumes.

Britain's BS 1928:1955 adopted characteristics identical to RIAA in 1955.14 Germany took longer: DIN 45536/45537 of 1959 used a CCIR-based curve with RIAA's bass turnovers but 50 microseconds of treble pre-emphasis, and in 1962 CCIR and DIN moved to 75 microseconds, making the curve identical to RIAA.14

When did stereo records come out?#

The stereo LP is a two-channel trick inside a single groove (playing explains how a stylus reads it), and it went from a lab to an American standard in under six months. Westrex's experimental 45/45 cutter, which carves the left and right channels on the two walls of one groove, each at 45 degrees, dates to July 12, 1957. It was shown to engineers at Westrex's Hollywood lab on August 26, demonstrated at the Audio Engineering Society's ninth convention in New York on October 11, and adopted by the Record Industry Association of America as the US standard on December 27, 1957.15

The first single-groove stereo demo disc reached the public by mail. Audio Fidelity's AFLP 1872 came from masters Westrex cut in October 1957, and in mid-December the label advertised in Billboard that it would send a copy to anyone who wrote on company letterhead; Copper Magazine puts the pressing at 500 copies.16 Stereo itself was older than the groove: Alan Blumlein's stereo patent applications at EMI date to 1931, and Emory Cook issued stereo reel-to-reel tape in 1951 and later two-groove stereo discs that needed two cartridges to play.16

The first four stereo discs on general sale came from Audio Fidelity in March 1958, and the label had four more out by the end of that month.17

When did vinyl die?#

It never quite did, but it came close. In US dollar terms, the LP and EP peaked at 62.7% of the market in 1977. Cassettes passed them in 1983 (47.8% against 44.6%) and reached 55.3% in 1985. CDs went from 0.5% in 1983 to 95.5% at their peak in 2002. The LP and EP fell to 1.1% in 1990 and stayed below 0.3% from 1992 to 2007.18

In units, US vinyl shipments more than halved between 1984 and 1988. In 1991 the US shipped 22 million vinyl singles and 4.8 million LPs and EPs; by 1995 it was 10.2 million singles and 2.2 million LPs and EPs. LP shipments had a small 1990s high of 3.4 million in 1998, then fell to 900,000 in 2006, the bottom; vinyl singles bottomed at 300,000 in 2009.19 SoundScan (now Luminate), which counts sales to consumers rather than shipments, put the low a year earlier, at 857,000 vinyl albums in 2005.19 Worldwide, IFPI figures fall from 450 million vinyl albums in 1989 to 3 million in 2006.19 In the UK, just over 200,000 LPs were bought in 2007.20

The factories went with the sales. After the CD arrived, major labels sold, scrapped and dismantled their pressing machines.21 Around 2015 the US had about 16 plants still running; the largest operated 28 presses and was buying used ones from closed plants as far away as Italy.22

How did the revival start?#

Part of it was a holiday invented by shop owners. Record Store Day was conceived in September 2007; Eric Levin of Criminal Records presented the idea, modeled partly on Free Comic Book Day, with Michael Kurtz and Carrie Colliton among the co-founders. The first one was held in April 2008, launched with Metallica signing at Rasputin Music.23

The sales figures tell the rest. The RIAA's 2025 year-end report describes "19 YEARS of vinyl growth" in the US, which puts the turn right around the 2006 low.24 Demand outran the rebuilt factories: by 2022 the AP counted about 40 US plants, mostly small, backlogs of six to eight months, and only a handful of companies worldwide making presses, "none in the U.S."21

How a record is cut

The short version: Every pressed record starts as a groove carved in real time by a heated stylus on a lathe, usually into a lacquer-coated aluminum disc, one side at a time.3334 The cut obeys physics a streaming master never meets: bass has to stay centered, harsh "s" sounds have to be tamed, and every extra minute on a side costs level, bass or both.3536 The machines are mostly decades old, with a couple of new ones arriving since 2023, and the playback curve that undoes the cutting EQ has been standard since 1954.373839

Do I need a separate master for vinyl?#

Usually, yes, because a groove is a physical object with a stereo problem built in. The mid signal (what the left and right channels share) moves the groove side to side, and the side signal (the difference between them) moves it up and down.40 Big low-frequency differences between channels mean big vertical swings, and if the two channels are out of phase the groove can narrow until a stylus cannot track it, or the cutter head lifts off the lacquer altogether.4135

Gotta Groove, a US plant, says it bluntly in its mastering notes: "Always center your bass frequencies."35 Hard-panned bass, kick or toms can make a record skip or skate.35 Engineers typically keep the low end in mono below a certain frequency, and on long sides they often roll off bass as well, trading low end and stereo width for time.36

There is no single number. Writing for iZotope in 2019, mastering engineer Jett Galindo pointed out that bass-to-mono tools typically offer crossover settings from 20 Hz to 700 Hz, and that many vinyl cutters already consider 100 Hz excessive.42

The same plant lists the other usual trouble:35

Veteran cutting engineer Richard Simpson ranks sibilance as one of the two big cutting problems, causing distortion and tracking trouble on both the lacquer and playback.41 He also dissents a little: skipping the low-end roll-off or elliptical EQ (the filter that sums bass to mono) often sounded fuller to him, so "mono the bass" is a strong default rather than a law.41 He remembers RCA Hollywood's "Motown filter" rolling off everything below 70 Hz and above 12 kHz.41

Why does the end of a side sound worse?#

A record spins at a constant speed, so the groove under the stylus moves fastest at the rim and slowest near the label. A 12-inch LP at 33 1/3 rpm runs at "almost 21ips" at the outer edge and about 7.4 inches per second near the center.43 The same waveform has to squeeze into roughly a third of the groove length, so wavelengths shrink and get harder to cut and to track. This is inner groove distortion, worst on loud passages with lots of high-frequency energy, and a pivoting tonearm's tracking error also grows toward the center.44 Soundfly calls it "diameter loss."40

The fixes are all planning: quieter songs at the end of a side, shorter sides, or an album split across two discs.44 Or, in Soundfly's version, put the ballad last.40

Is 45 rpm better than 33 1/3?#

On the numbers, a 12-inch at 45 rpm moves about 28.3 inches per second at the outer edge, "close to the professional tape standard of 30ips."43 More groove per second means more room for each wavelength, and HiFi News calls higher-speed formats "almost always sonically superior."43 The cost is time: a 45 rpm side holds far less music, as the next table shows. Whether a given listener hears the difference is a separate argument.

How much music fits on one side?#

Less than most labels would like, and it depends on the music. Groove width and depth decide how much signal fits. A shorter side allows deeper, wider grooves with more dynamic range, bass and level; a longer side means lower level, a higher noise floor and tighter spacing, which raises the risk of overcuts (grooves running into each other, causing skips) and, on lacquer, pre-echo and post-echo, faint ghosts of a loud passage heard right before or after it.36 A louder cut also hides surface noise better.36

Side-length check

Will it fit on one side?

Cutting engineers trade loudness and bass for time: the longer the side, the closer the grooves and the quieter the cut. These are plants' recommended limits, not hard rules - ask your cutting engineer.

What is a lacquer?#

The lacquer (or master disc) is an aluminum plate with a thick coat of lacquer, and the engineer cuts it in real time with a stylus that is usually sapphire.33 There is one lacquer per side.34 The gaps between songs are made by nudging the stylus slightly toward the center, and the engineer adds the lead-in, lead-out and the crossover grooves by hand before checking each lacquer under a microscope for overcuts.3334

The supply of blank lacquers is thinner than most buyers would guess. On February 6, 2020, a fire destroyed the Apollo Masters factory in Banning, California, which made an estimated 70 to 85 percent of the world's lacquers and was one of only two such factories; MDC in Japan was left as the only producer.46 After the fire, DJ Mag heard that MDC was basically closed to new business and that at least three groups were working on new lacquer manufacturing; one industry source said only two companies were known to still make gem-tipped cutting styli.47 Some plants moved to DMM (below), and the Vinyl Record Manufacturers Association of North America formed in response.46

New blanks have proved hard to make. Gotta Groove Records spent 2017 to 2022 working with Sherwin-Williams on lacquer blanks of its own and gave up, mainly because the discs were so hard to cure; as of 2026 it still recommends MDC blanks from Japan.48

Why is the cutting stylus heated?#

Heat is what made quiet, detailed lacquers possible. In 1948 William S. Bachman at Columbia wrapped a copper wire coil around the stylus and heated it with direct current; the warmth softened the lacquer, allowed much smaller burnishing facets (the tiny flats that polish the groove wall) and kept more high-frequency detail.37 The method was published in Audio Engineering in June 1950, and Fairchild later sold a "thermostylus" kit with a claimed noise reduction of about 20 dB (a manufacturer's claim).37 The hot stylus matters beyond the cutting room: the RIAA curve's 75 microsecond treble setting relied on it.37

What is a mastering lathe, and who made them?#

The lathe turns the blank and moves the cutter head, which controls the cutting stylus, across it.49 Two names dominate the history.

Scully. John J. Scully built a weight-driven lathe in 1921, and Western Electric bought one in 1924 to demonstrate its Westrex cutter.49 Scully dominated the US professional market from the 1930s to the 1960s.49 It lost ground once Neumann lathes were imported, its new design did not arrive until the mid-1970s, and it was often fitted with Westrex cutter heads and electronics.50

Neumann. The AM31 arrived in 1931, followed by the VMS66 in 1966, the VMS70 in 1970 and the VMS80 in 1980.49 Pspatial Audio calls the VMS 70 the world's most popular recording lathe and says Neumann was the only company selling lathe, cutter head and electronics as one system, at more than $100,000 in today's money by its estimate.50 Neumann lathes from 1931 to 1980 used Lyrec synchronous AC motors whose speed followed the mains frequency; the VMS-80 dropped the traditional bed and used DC servo motors, and in 1989 the last remaining disk-lathe maker assembled a VMS-82 DMM lathe in Berlin.51 Pspatial dates Neumann's switch to crystal-locked Technics DC motors to after 1976.50

On heads, Ortofon made its first moving-coil cutter head in 1946, and Western Electric/Westrex showed the first commercial 45/45 stereo cutter head in 1957.5049 Neumann's SX-74 is a stereo feedback head; the SX-84, developed with Teldec for DMM, fits only the VMS-82.51

Does anyone still build lathes?#

When the old line ended depends on whom you read. In 2019 Mix wrote that "no manufacturer in the world has made one since the early 1980s," in a profile of a Nashville rebuild of a 1940s Scully 501: about $3,000 in 2014 for the bathtub, sled and original platter, $25,000 to $30,000 in parts overall, and roughly four years of work, finished in summer 2018.52 Copper Magazine has a VMS-82 built in Berlin in 1989.51

New machines have arrived since. Agnew Analog unveiled the Type 612 at the International Disk Recording and Mastering Convention in July 2023 and listed it for order by August; it cuts lacquers, dubplates or plastics, has a hydraulic carriage, takes a 12- or 14-inch vacuum platter and accepts any cutter head, including Agnew's own Type 602.38 In July 2026 MusicTech reported the Teenage Engineering APC-2, built with SUPERSENSE, a 104 kg made-to-order lathe that cuts playback discs in real time; no price or ship date yet.39

What is groove pitch, and what does variable pitch do?#

Pitch is groove spacing, measured in lines per inch.50 A fixed pitch wastes space on quiet passages and risks overcuts on loud ones. Variable pitch solves this by reading the music about a second early: on tape-era Neumann systems a preview head sat about 38 cm ahead of the playback head, so the lathe could widen the spacing before a loud or bass-heavy passage and tighten it during a quiet one.50 Neumann paired it with dynamic depth control driven by the side (L-R) signal, and an "ECHO" button widened the pitch to cut down pre-echo.50 Tight spacing on long sides is what raises the overcut and echo risk in the first place.36

Scully introduced variable pitch in 1950 and automated it in 1955.49 When Neumann got it is murkier. Wikipedia has it arriving with the VMS80;49 Pspatial Audio says the VMS 66 and VMS 70 already had a separate pitch-control console fed by the preview head.50

What does DMM mean on a record?#

Direct Metal Mastering skips the lacquer. A diamond stylus, excited at an ultrasonic frequency so it cuts more easily, carves the groove straight into amorphous copper electroplated onto a 0.8 mm stainless steel disc, with no heated stylus needed.53 The cut disc becomes the "mother" for plating, which skips the silvering and nickel steps a lacquer needs.53 Teldec claims no lacquer springback, no pre- or post-echo, 10 to 15 percent more playing time per side, up to 6 dB less high-frequency noise and stampers in about two hours, and pitched it as economical for short runs (all manufacturer claims).53 With no springback, Teldec says, the grooves can also sit closer together.53

DMM came from Teldec (Telefunken-Decca) and Neumann. About 30 DMM lathes were ever made, about 10 were later converted to lacquer, and as of 2009 seven public DMM facilities remained, in Germany, the Netherlands and the Czech Republic; the US lost its last in 2005.54 The DMM logo was tied to Teldec's specification while it enforced it; after the patents expired the name and logo were unrestricted.51

The sound is argued over. Some listeners hear DMM as bright or forward, which some put down to the hard copper and a near-zero cutting angle that needs electronic correction.54 In 2020 DJ Mag noted doubts about DMM for dancefloor releases, and Richard Dron, founder of the pressing company Mobineko, said he did not love its sound, while noting that big European plants such as GZ and Optimal relied mostly on it.47

A 1987 Audio magazine feature dates DMM's introduction to 1981 and points out that Teldec had used the underlying process for its TED videodisc, introduced in 1976.55 Teldec's US patent for DMM was filed on April 7, 1982.56

What is the RIAA curve?#

Left alone, bass would carve enormous swings and treble would barely scratch the surface. The RIAA curve fixes this: the cutting side cuts bass and boosts treble, and the playback side does the exact opposite, so the overall response comes out flat.57 It roughly approximates a constant-amplitude groove, so no part of the band hogs the disc, and it improves signal-to-noise.57 The playback half of that curve, plus a lot of gain, is the phono preamp's job (see playing); history tells how the labels settled on one curve.

The numbers are three time constants, 3180, 318 and 75 microseconds, which put the corners at 50.05 Hz, 500.5 Hz and 2122 Hz.57 A true constant-amplitude cut would need about 60 dB more gain at 20 kHz than at 20 Hz; RIAA asks for about 40 dB.57 The curve dates from 1954, after the 1953 NARTB standard, replacing what Stereophile calls "an anarchy of different EQs."3757 It became part of IEC 98, first published in 1964; in September 1976 the IEC added a fourth time constant (7950 microseconds, 20.02 Hz) to roll off infrasonic rumble, and critics say that adds audible errors of -3.0 dB at 20 Hz, -1.0 dB at 40 Hz and -0.5 dB at 60 Hz.57

What is half-speed mastering?#

Run the source and the lathe at half speed, so an LP is cut at about 16 2/3 rpm, and the stylus gets twice the time for every wiggle, which makes high frequencies easier to cut.58 The lathe's EQ has to be modified or the curve lands in the wrong frequency range, and each album needs an acetate check, so a cut takes about four times as long.58 Stan Ricker cut half-speed LPs for Mobile Fidelity Sound Lab, which licensed classic albums and had them pressed in Japan.58 At Abbey Road in London, Miles Showell, who joined in 2013, cuts half-speed masters from high-resolution digital transfers on a modified Neumann and calls the method the "holy grail of vinyl cutting."5859

What do one-step and three-step mean?#

After cutting, a lacquer is silvered and electroplated with nickel. The nickel peeled off is a negative called the father; plating the father makes a positive mother, and the mother makes stampers, the plates that press the records.3334 Disc Makers describes three routes:34

Process Chain Used for
One-step First plate off the lacquer is the stamper Very small orders
Two-step Stamper is plated again to keep a mother Future stampers
Three-step Father to several mothers to several stampers each Runs of tens of thousands or more

Yamaha puts it at one stamper per thousand records, though real stamper life varies.33 DMM needs only one electroforming stage, since the copper disc is already the mother.5453

On audiophile reissues, "One-Step" is also a selling point. Mobile Fidelity's UltraDisc One-Step pressings use stampers made straight from the lacquers, skipping the father and mother, which the label says avoids the generational losses of three-step plating.60 Since each lacquer yields one stamper, more lacquers have to be cut.60 The sonic benefit is the label's claim.

What is a lathe-cut record?#

A lathe cut is made one at a time in real time, the groove engraved straight into a blank by a diamond or sapphire stylus, with no plating and no press.61 Freestyle Vinyl, which sells them, says lathe cuts make economic sense from one copy up to "a few dozen," while pressing pays off from hundreds up; blanks include vinyl, polycarbonate and acrylic.61 It also allows that they can sound more raw or lo-fi than pressed records, with quality varying by equipment, material and mastering, and that vinyl lathe cuts may outlast polycarbonate or acrylic ones, though it gives no play count.62 New professional lathes cover this ground too: Agnew lists "plastics" among what the Type 612 can cut.38

Where are records cut today?#

The cutting trade is small and scattered: the last public DMM rooms (as of 2009) were in central and northern Europe, and after February 2020 the only lacquer maker left standing was in Japan.5446 Denmark has RPM Records, and London has Abbey Road, the best-known home of half-speed cutting.3659 Mobile Fidelity's early half-speed LPs were pressed in Japan.58

Track 3

How a record is pressed

The short version: A pressed record is a copy of a copy of a copy. The cut lacquer is silvered like a mirror, grown into nickel negatives and positives in a plating bath, and the last generation, the stamper, is mounted in a press that squeezes a warm lump of PVC at something like 100 tons for about half a minute.6333 Many plants keep old presses running on salvaged parts, the compound and lacquer supply has hung on very few suppliers, and the 2020 fire at a California lacquer plant plus a demand surge produced waits of up to 10 months.64654666 By early 2025 typical backlogs were down to about three months.64

How do you get from a soft lacquer to a metal stamper?#

The lacquer that leaves the cutting room is an aluminum disc coated in nitrocellulose, with the groove cut into that coating.67 Nothing can be pressed from it until it becomes metal, and the first step is borrowed from mirror-making: the lacquer is sprayed with a saponin mix, rinsed, sensitized with stannous chloride, rinsed again, then sprayed with a silver solution and a dextrose reducer.63 The silver makes the surface conductive so it can be electroplated, usually with nickel.63

Most plants start with a nickel "flash" in a slow, warm bath at about 15 amps, then move to a hot nickel bath and raise the current in steps to between 110 and 200 amps.63 Nickel sulfamate baths made all-nickel parts possible; from 1940 to 1960 a short nickel plate was backed with copper.63

What peels off the lacquer is a negative: where the record has a valley, this part has a ridge. It is called the master (the UK term) or the father.6368 A mother, a positive with grooves like a playable record, is plated from the father in about an hour, and a stamper, another negative weighing about 145 grams, is plated from the mother in about 45 minutes.63 A new mother is polished to round off microscopic "horns" at the tops of the grooves so the vinyl flows during pressing.63 The finished stamper is chrome plated for a hard surface, center-punched (by aligning it over three pins or by watching the grooves under a microscope), trimmed, sanded on the back, and given a clamping lip at the edge.63

What do one-step, two-step and three-step mean?#

They count the generations of metal between the lacquer and the press. At one US plant, one-step means the first metal part off the lacquer is itself the stamper, so no more stampers can ever be made from that cut - generally viewed as inferior, the plant says. In two-step, the first part is plated into a mother and stampers come from the mother; in three-step, the mother is plated again into an archive copy, and dozens of mothers can come from one master.69

The names are not used consistently. That plant calls the archive copy the "father," while a large Nashville plant and Yamaha use "father" for the first negative off the lacquer, which Wikipedia calls the master.69683363 The Nashville plant describes its own method as a "3-step electroplating process": lacquer, then father, then mothers, then stampers.68 If a plant quote lists steps, ask which part they mean.

Direct Metal Mastering (DMM) skips the lacquer and its plating by cutting straight into copper. It is said to lower surface noise, though some doubt its range for dance releases.47 After the 2020 lacquer fire, some plants turned to DMM, a costlier route.46 How a record is cut covers how it works.

How many records can one stamper press?#

Nobody publishes a standard. Yamaha's 2021 explainer says "one stamper for every thousand records."33 A 2025 Hypebot piece by Bobby Owsinski says a stamper "may last for as few as 2,000 records."64 Gotta Groove says its coated stampers, developed in 2017-2018 with a coatings firm, double the life of standard ones in most cases; it has pressed "a few records of over 3,000 copies" on one set, which it calls outliers, and says a late-2018 test of a 180 gram box set found copies 45 and 2,400 very similar.69 Those are the plant's own claims. A Dallas plant's FAQ warns that very large runs risk stamper wear unless new stampers are made.70

Because new stampers come off the mother, a long run never wears out the cut itself.63

What is a test pressing?#

The first few records off new stampers, made before the full order. Vinyl Moon put the usual batch at four or five in 2018; Yamaha says "a few."7133 The artist and label listen and approve, and if they hear serious problems the plant may have to start over.71 One plant listens to every test pressing in full before shipping, reports a 98% approval rate (its own figure), and in production listens to parts of every 25th record, with one listening room for every two presses.72 Another compares production copies against the approved test pressing.68

It is the cheapest moment to catch a problem: only a handful of copies exist.

What happens inside the press?#

PVC compound is warmed and pushed through an extruder, "squeezed like toothpaste," as a Third Man staffer put it to Tape Op in 2018, into a lump called a puck or biscuit.73 The biscuit is about three times as thick as the finished record.71 A label goes on each side, the biscuit sits between the two stampers, steam heats the mold, the press closes, cold water cools it, and the ring of excess called flash is trimmed off.7371 Flash and some rejects get recycled.6871

Labels are pressed into the vinyl as the stampers close, and they are baked beforehand to drive out moisture so they don't bubble.33 One plant bakes them overnight so they adhere properly and warns that the colors will shift a little.74 A 2019 patent application for a "no-bake" label ties wrinkling and lifting to heat, and says keeping the label's backing free of water helps stop it sticking to the press plates.75

Which presses are still running, and who makes new ones?#

The classic makes still at work or rebuilt include SMT, Toolex Alpha and Finebilt; a Kansas plant rebuilt one of each and counts them among the best brands ever made.79 A large Nashville plant says it runs more than 60 presses (Lened, Pheenix Alpha and SMT) making "up to 80,000 records daily," a company claim.68 For years plants kept presses alive with salvaged parts and hunted used machines in warehouses.64

New machines arrived in the 2010s. Newbilt in Germany builds semi-automatic presses based on the Finebilt design, which stopped being made in the early 1960s, and a full line was listed at about 200,000 euros (company figure, year not stated).80 Third Man Pressing in Detroit bought eight of the first ten and told Tape Op in 2018 it expected about 5,000 records a shift.73 Viryl in Toronto claimed in 2016 that its automatic WarmTone could press a 140 gram record in 20 to 25 seconds and that one operator could run four.78 By 2018 Viryl offered a "steamless" version that heats mold water electrically, removing the gas boiler.77 Pheenix Alpha in Sweden builds the automatic AD12, credits Toolex Alpha with developing it, and announced a manual version in March 2025.81

What is vinyl compound, and where does it come from?#

Records are pressed from a PVC compound sold as pellets. At one Thai maker, PVC powder is melted, extruded, chopped, cleaned (metal contamination included) and tested, and the plant turns out the pellets.82 Black comes from carbon black, which also strengthens the record; colored compound uses dyes and other additives.83 Panelists at Making Vinyl Europe 2024 said lead and similar additives were phased out of record PVC by 2000.66

In 2014, Thai Plastic & Chemicals supplied up to 90% of the PVC compound used for records, and the last US supplier, Keysor-Century, had closed in the mid-2000s.65 By 2020, DJ Mag counted "at least a dozen" suppliers of audio-grade PVC.47

A 2016 Thermo Fisher materials blog describes PVC blends as "complex mixtures of PVC particles, fillers, lubricants, stabilizers and plasticizers."84 No current figure for any supplier's share of record compound is public.

What happened to lacquer supply after the 2020 fire?#

On February 6, 2020, fire destroyed Apollo Masters in Banning, California, the only US maker of lacquer blanks; 82 firefighters worked about three hours, nobody was hurt, and the cause is undetermined.46 Apollo made an estimated 70-85% of the world's lacquers, and its loss left one known lacquer factory: MDC, a small Japanese company.46 MDC had a fixed supply and was said to be closed to new business, and three outfits were developing new lacquer production.47 Some plants moved to DMM, backlogs grew, and the fire led to the founding of the Vinyl Record Manufacturers Association of North America (VRMA), with about 50 member companies.46

Every 2020 report names MDC. A 2024 conference report in Copper names "the Japanese supplier, Public Records" as the only one left after the fire.66 None of the new lacquer projects is confirmed to have shipped.

In a September 2026 history post, the US plant Gotta Groove says it "continues to recommend master lacquer blanks produced by the Public Record Company in Japan," and calls those blanks "MDC's."48 So MDC and Public Record are most likely one Japanese maker under two names, though nobody says so outright.

Why did records take so long to make in 2021?#

Demand outran capacity. In November 2021, Mixmag reported lead times of about three months to a year, with some small artists told they might wait until summer 2022, and cited Billboard's estimate of about 160 million records a year of global capacity against demand of about 400 million.85 Adele's 30 was reported at more than 500,000 vinyl copies, and Ed Sheeran said she "had basically booked out all the vinyl factories."85

By May 2023 most supply problems were easing, and a Dallas plant that had doubled its lines was running "24 hours, five days a week."86 At Making Vinyl Europe 2024, industry people said the pandemic waits of up to 10 months were over.66 In January 2025 the backlog at most plants was about three months.64 Plants now quote "weeks to months," depending on season and load.70

How many plants are there, and which is the biggest?#

The Vinyl Factory counted 40 vinyl record manufacturers worldwide in 2014, with the US and Europe home to more than 80%; FACT put US plants at about 15 that year.8765 There is no current worldwide count. FutureSource reported 110 million units pressed in 2023, from a survey that covered only about a third of known plants.66

Where else in the world are records pressed?#

GZ Media, in Lodenice near Prague, is the world's largest by most accounts. In 2022 it told Radio Prague it pressed more than 55 million records in 2021, almost 200,000 a day around the clock, against about 300,000 a year in the mid-1990s; it runs eight plants, including sites in Canada, Memphis, Nashville and France, builds its own presses, and pressed its first record in 1951 (company figures).88 Newcomers in 2024 included a press project in Navi Mumbai, India, which pressed what was billed as the country's first record in decades, a broker's plan to press K-pop in South Korea, a plant in Glasgow, and a project in Israel halted by the conflict.66 Parts suppliers keep the old presses going: one US company that has served the record industry since 1946 sells machinery and parts for SMT, Hamilton, Alpha-Toolex, Finebilt, Newbilt and Lenned presses.89

Sizes, speeds, weights and colors

The short version: A 12 inch at 33 1/3 rpm holds about 18 to 22 minutes a side, more if the music is quiet; spin the same disc at 45 and you trade minutes for a faster groove.3543 A 180 gram record is pressed from the same stampers, with the same groove depth, as a standard one of about 140 grams, and the people who build turntables can't agree on whether the extra weight does anything for the sound.9091 Colored, splatter and picture discs are made differently from black and can be noisier, and the new PET "eco" records arrive with claims that only their makers have tested.7092

Why does a 45 sound different from a 33?#

The groove moves faster under the stylus. On a 12 inch at 33 1/3 rpm it passes at "almost 21ips" (inches per second) at the outer edge and about 7.4 ips near the label. At 45 the outer edge runs about 28.3 ips, "close to the professional tape standard of 30ips."43 HiFi News calls higher-speed formats "almost always sonically superior."43 The cost is time.

Groove speed falls toward the center of every record, so high frequencies get harder to reproduce as a side goes on. Sequence with that in mind; it is no accident how many sides end on a ballad.40

Why do some records say 180 gram?#

A 180 gram record is a heavier 12 inch LP. Standard weight is "around 140 grams" to Disc Makers and "between 130 and 155 grams" to Gotta Groove, and the final number shifts with the vinyl color and the length of the program.9072

The weight doesn't change the groove. A 180 gram record uses the same stampers and the same groove depth as a standard one, and Disc Makers says that on most systems the two sound the same.90 When What Hi-Fi? put the question to turntable and cartridge makers in 2025, the answers split three ways:91

  • It's the cut, not the weight. Rega's Simon Webster said groove depth is set by the cutting engineer and "you can have excellent 180g pressings and poor 180g pressings." DUAL's Lothar Mertens said 120 gram and 180 gram copies from the same master can sound identical. Clearaudio's Robert Suchy said heavy vinyl "can't make a poor recording sound good."
  • Weight can help. Audio-Technica's Edward Forth said less vibration reaches the stylus, and Suchy said resonance may be lower. Webster said heavier records last longer and warp less.
  • Weight has its own problems. Forth said a warped 180 gram disc can't be rescued the way a 140 gram one can. Ortofon's Leif Johannsen said thicker discs need careful cooling after pressing or they warp. Technics' Tetsuya Itani said, from personal experience, that they may pick up more static.

The weight is printed on the sticker. The quality of the cut and the pressing isn't.

Does colored vinyl sound worse than black?#

Colored records are pressed from colored PVC pellets. Black gets its color from carbon black, which also strengthens the record; colored compound uses dyes and other additives instead.7183 Hand Drawn Pressing in Dallas says colored and picture discs "can introduce higher surface noise if not handled properly," and that black virgin vinyl "typically remains the lowest-noise option."70 Listeners are less worried: many say translucent vinyl sounds almost the same as black, though some warn off white.83 Nobody has published measurements, so the plant's "real but manageable" is the firmest word going.

Splatter comes from mixing pellets of different colors in one biscuit. Plants also hand-mix biscuits for color-in-color and A-side/B-side effects.7196

How is a picture disc made?#

A picture disc starts as a PVC core with a printed paper or card image on each side. Atlas Records, a UK shop, says the grooves are pressed into a thin clear polyethylene foil laid over the top, and the layers are pressed together and trimmed; the foil wears faster than regular vinyl, and picture discs tend to cost more.97 Unified Manufacturing, a Los Angeles CD, vinyl and merch company, describes the same sandwich with a clear PVC layer on the outside and no foil.98 Neither presses records itself, so the outer layer's material is still an open question.

Are there eco-friendly records that aren't PVC?#

There are, and every performance figure so far comes from the makers or the trade press.

  • EcoRecord. Sonopress in Gutersloh, Germany, injection-molds recyclable PET instead of compression-molding PVC, on a machine of up to 300 tons, with no gas or steam, and claims energy savings of up to 85% in a test run.92 It launched with Warner Music; the first order was an orange Liam Gallagher and John Squire release at US $44.01 (2024, official retail price).92 The From Vinyl to Plastic writer wanted someone independent to check the sound claims.92 Bobby Owsinski reported in 2025 that surface noise was lower by up to 10 dB, but that wear showed after about 35 plays against about 50 for standard vinyl, and the discs cost more.64 At Making Vinyl 2024, about 70% of each EcoRecord was said to come from bottles collected from a river in Guatemala in 2023, nine bottles per record.66
  • Electric injection elsewhere. Green Vinyl Records swapped gas-powered presses for an electric injection-molding machine, and PVC for virgin and recycled PET, by an equipment supplier's account, which gives no figures or dates.99
  • Bio-plastic and recycled PVC. Evolution Music in the UK has made a record from sugars and starches, and Elastic Stage is working on something similar.64 Beologic regrinds old records with the labels punched out; its own research says seven in 10 listeners preferred the recycled pressings.66
  • The cost question. At the 2024 conference MPO asked whether labels could absorb "25 to 30 percent more" for PVC alternatives.66
  • The rules. VRMA and the Vinyl Alliance offered six anti-greenwashing principles in 2024, among them truthful claims, a full life-cycle view, and evidence.66

None of this is new in spirit. In the mid-1960s Emory Cook ran a vacuum "microfusion" process using micro-particles of vinyl, and claimed lower noise for it.76 The planet covers the footprint numbers.

The runout groove: what the etchings mean

The short version: The smooth band between the last song and the label is called the dead wax or runout, and it carries the record's paperwork: a matrix number for each side, cut numbers, stamper codes, plant marks and often the initials of the engineer who cut it.100 Read the right way, those scratches can tell you which factory pressed your copy and which lacquer it came from, but no single mark proves a first pressing on its own.100101 And every so often there is a joke in there too.102

What is the dead wax, and why is anything written there?#

The matrix number is a code, letters and numbers and sometimes symbols, stamped or handwritten in the runout groove area, which also goes by dead wax, end-groove area or matrix area.100 It exists for the factory, to file the stamper and make sure each side gets the right label. Collectors borrowed it later to tell editions apart.100 Around that core number you can find a cut or take number, plant codes or logos, the cutting engineer's initials or signature, and sometimes dates.100

Don't confuse it with the catalog number, the bigger number that is usually the same on both labels. Matrix numbers differ side to side, and some labels print them upside down to avoid exactly that confusion.100

There is a geography to it. On British records the lacquer number normally sits at 6 o'clock, with matrix and stamper information 90 degrees away at 9, 12 or 3 o'clock.103 Run a fingernail over them: stamper codes are embossed and stand proud of the surface, while lacquer and mother codes are carved in.103 On Contemporary Records' jazz runouts, the style of the stamps tracks the plating plant and the era, not the cutting room.104

The habit is old. Before about 1950, records were cut direct to disc and take numbers were inscribed in the matrix area; on Victor records the take number sits at "9 o'clock".100 In the 1950s, one engineer recalls, every cutter at Fine Sound had a personal "maker's mark" that he inscribed, and dedicated mastering houses later stamped their own imprints.105

What do A1, B1, -1A and RE actually mean?#

The number after the dash is usually the cut. A suffix such as 12345-3 marks the third cut of that side, and an album numbered X-1234 might carry X-1234-B7 in the runout, the 7 being the cut number.100 Records get recut for ordinary reasons: stampers wear out or get damaged, a test pressing is rejected, a fault turns up in the banding, another plant or country cuts its own masters, or a reissue gets a new number.100 Sometimes the old cut is reused with the new number added and the old one scratched out.100

Vinyl Me, Please says a "-1A" suffix "typically" indicates a first pressing of side A, and "RE" a repressing.106 A UK shop blog lists "A1 B1" (also written A//1 B//1 or A-1 B-1) as a common first-press code.107 Both are good starting points, and neither is a rule. The clearest proof comes from inside Columbia.

A former Columbia cutting engineer, Phil Brown, told AnalogPlanet that an initial lacquer order was six sets: 1A and 1B went to the Pitman plant, 1C and 1D to Terre Haute, 1E and 1F to Santa Maria.101 All six are first cuttings, so the collector's belief that A came first, B second and C third is wrong.101 Each plant got two sets because lacquers could be lost in plating. A change of program moved the number to -2, then -3, and if the first lacquer was ruined, the next cut could be 2A and be treated as the first.101

Two more cautions: a cut 1 may have been rejected, and reissues often restart at 1.100 A "-RE" suffix doesn't necessarily mean the non-RE version was ever sold to the public.100 Letters can mean people, too. In a lacquer number such as "AM 438-1", a letter suffix like -1R can identify the mastering engineer at that firm.103

What does DMM mean in the dead wax?#

DMM, Direct Metal Mastering, means the record was cut straight into a copper-plated master rather than a lacquer.54 Teldec and Georg Neumann GmbH developed it, Neumann building the cutters into its VMS80 lathe series, and the idea came from RCA's late-1970s videodisc work.54 The payoff is in the factory: one electroplating stage to make stampers instead of three for lacquer, and in the early 1980s many copies could be pressed from one master without wear-driven recuts.54100 You will see "DMM" on the sleeve or inscribed in the lead-out area.54

The sound is argued about; some listeners hear DMM pressings as harsh or forward in the treble.54

Only about 30 DMM lathes were made, and about 10 of those were converted to lacquer. As of 2009, seven publicly usable DMM rooms remained, in Germany, the Netherlands and the Czech Republic, and the US had lost its last in 2005.54 Masterdisk's user-written Discogs profile tells a different story: DMM cutting since February 1986, still listed among its services.117 Both can't be current, and nobody has settled which is.

How do I use all this to identify my pressing?#

  1. Copy the runout exactly. Discogs asks contributors to enter it verbatim, one string per side, keeping capitalization, single spaces for gaps and strikethrough for crossed-out text.118 Then search Discogs by the full string.109
  2. Sort raised from carved. Embossed codes come from the stamper; carved ones from the lacquer or mother.103
  3. Find the plant. A letter or logo usually does it. If there is none, measure the pressing ring on the label, which differs by plant: about 70mm at Specialty, 36mm at Capitol Winchester, a double 35/72mm at Monarch.110
  4. Look for the lowest cut and stamper numbers. The lowest indicators, such as -A, -1 or -1S, point to earlier copies.109 Keep the Columbia caveat in mind: 1A to 1F were all first cuttings sent to different plants.101
  5. Check the paper too. Label designs date a pressing: the Warner Bros. green label ran from about 1970 to 1973, then the "Burbank" palm-tree label from 1973 to 1978.110 A barcode on a 1965 title means a reissue, since barcodes were not widely used before the late 1970s.109
  6. Cross-check. Discogs has errors, so check forums and specialist sites as well.110

On CDs the same instinct applies with different codes. Discogs separates the L-prefixed mastering SID, added at glass mastering, from the IFPI mould SID added at pressing, and notes that an "LC" code is a label code, not a matrix or catalog number.118

Track 6

How a turntable plays a record

The short version: A diamond the size of a grain of sand rides a wiggling groove, and a magnet and a coil turn that wiggle into a tiny signal that a phono preamp lifts and corrects. Most skips, hiss, lopsided stereo and worn records come down to a few settings and storage habits. Playing a record does wear it, though the two classic lab studies disagree about what happens to the groove.

How does one groove carry two channels?#

A stereo LP has one groove with two walls, and the two recording directions sit at 90 degrees to each other and at 45 degrees to the record surface.119 Put a signal on one channel alone and only one wall is modulated; the other stays smooth.119 That is the 45/45 system, which Westrex showed to engineers in Hollywood on August 26, 1957, and which the Record Industry Association of America adopted "as the American standard" on December 27, 1957.15 A rival vertical-lateral system had backing from Sugden in the UK and Telefunken in Germany, and Decca was experimenting with it too.15 One reason 45/45 won: both channels were cut the same way, so they used the same equalization and carried the same rumble, where a vertical system would have put more rumble in one channel.15 The history covers how stereo records reached the public.

In practice the stylus does not read two walls separately. A 1958 magazine explainer called that idea a misconception, since "it is impossible for the stylus to follow different contours on both walls of the groove at the same time."120 The tip moves in a mix of sideways (lateral) and up-and-down (vertical) motion, and real music traces angled, circular or elliptical paths.120

Which phase gives which motion is where the 1958 sources part ways. The Westrex engineers wrote that in-phase signals cut a vertical groove and out-of-phase signals a lateral one.119 Norman Crowhurst wrote the opposite: in-phase gives lateral motion, out-of-phase gives vertical.120 The split comes from how each defines channel phase. The usual modern explanation, that a mono signal moves the stylus purely sideways, follows Crowhurst.

Which wall is which is settled: the inner wall carries the left channel and the outer wall the right.121 The standard itself, IEC 60098, has a clause on channel phasing, but its wording isn't in the free preview.122

The design was built to stay compatible. An ordinary lateral pickup with decent vertical give would still play back the lateral part of both channels, Westrex wrote, and each 45/45 channel came out about 3 dB below a mono lateral record with the same playing time.119

What is the difference between moving magnet and moving coil?#

Both types make a signal by moving a magnet relative to a coil. In a moving magnet (MM) cartridge the magnet rides on the cantilever, the tiny arm that carries the stylus, and the coils stay put; in a moving coil (MC) cartridge it is the other way round.123 MC designs use fewer turns or thinner wire to keep the moving parts light, which is why their output is lower.123

How long does a stylus last?#

It depends on whose number you read. Audio-Technica rates its elliptical at about 300 hours,127 while Ortofon says the elliptical 2M Red can reach up to 1,000 hours with proper care "without degradation of performance."128 K&B Audio puts conical at about 500 hours, crediting Audio-Technica, which is the top of A-T's own range.126 House of Marley says styli usually last about 1,000 hours.130 Go by the hours your cartridge's maker publishes, and distrust any number that claims to cover every stylus.

How much should the stylus weigh on the record?#

Tracking force is the downward weight on the stylus, set with the tonearm's counterweight. Set it to the cartridge maker's figure: too light and the record skips, too heavy and it can damage the groove.130 The Ortofon 2M Red, for example, has a range of 1.6-2.0 g with 1.8 g recommended.128 Forces used to be much heavier. The 1958 Westrex pickup tracked at 6 g,119 and in 1968 about 5 g was common on everyday players.129

There is an engineering reason light tracking mistracks. Westrex wrote that the stylus's static deflection from tracking force has to be large compared with the biggest vertical modulation in the groove, or the tip leaves the groove.119

There is no single right number across makers. Audio-Technica says the recommended force "varies from cartridge to cartridge," and its 2015 setup guide gives 2 g for its AT95E and 3.5 g for its ATP-2XN.131

What does anti-skate actually do?#

A pivoted, offset tonearm has a quirk. Friction drags the stylus along the groove, but the arm can only resist force along its own length, and the leftover pulls it toward the center of the record.132 That is skating force. Louder passages and a dirty stylus or record mean more friction and more skating, and a 12-inch arm skates less than a shorter one.132 Left alone, the stylus leans on the inner groove wall, which can cause mistracking and uneven wear on both stylus and record.132 Anti-skate is an outward bias that cancels it.

How much is in dispute. Kogen measured skating at about 20% of tracking force for an elliptical and 15-18% for a conical in 1967; Boisclair estimated about 10%; Stereophile suggests starting near 10% and adjusting by ear.132 Then the advice conflicts. House of Marley says to set the anti-skate dial to match the tracking force, and to back it off if the stylus skips outward.130 Stereophile calls anti-skate equal to tracking force "way too high."132 Both can be right, because the dial is not a force gauge: Korf measured actual anti-skate at roughly 5-30% of the number on the dial.132 The Library of Congress says to check tracking and anti-skate at least monthly, or whenever mistracking shows up.133

Do I need a phono preamp?#

Yes, built in or separate. A cartridge's signal is far below line level, and the phono preamp (or phono stage) does two jobs: it adds gain, and it applies the inverse RIAA curve.124 A PHONO/LINE switch on a turntable turns its built-in stage on or off.124

The RIAA curve exists because of cutting. The lathe boosts treble and cuts bass so the groove's swing stays more even across the range, and playback does the reverse to get back to flat.57 The curve is defined by three time constants, 3180, 318 and 75 µs, with corners near 50 Hz, 500 Hz and 2,122 Hz.57 A true constant-amplitude cut would need about 60 dB more gain at 20 kHz than at 20 Hz; RIAA settles for about 40 dB.57 IEC 98 was first published in 1964, and a 1976 amendment added a 7950 µs infrasonic roll-off, -3.0 dB at 20 Hz, which drew criticism.57 Nearly all vinyl follows RIAA, apart from some pre-1954 records.124

The RIAA Technical Committee approved the curve on January 29, 1954, and the document circulated that June. The original 1954 text has not turned up.13

Does direct drive or belt drive matter?#

In direct drive the platter sits on the motor; belt drive turns it through a belt; idler drive uses a rubber wheel.134 For a listener at home, makers sell good decks of every type. For a DJ, direct drive changed everything: high torque, near-instant start, steady speed and a platter you can push around or spin backward without harm.135 Records and DJs picks up from there.

The first direct-drive deck was the Technics SP-10, designed by Shuichi Obata. IEEE Spectrum dates it to 1969;135 Technics says it launched in 1970 as "the world's first direct drive turntable."136

What do wow, flutter and rumble mean on a spec sheet?#

Wow is slow pitch wobble and flutter is fast pitch wobble; both are given as a percentage, and lower is better.134 Rumble is noise from the deck's own moving parts that the stylus picks up, and makers often leave it off.134 As a reference point, Panasonic lists the SL-1200MK7 at 0.025% wow and flutter (weighted RMS), 0.7 seconds to reach 33 1/3 rpm and 0.18 N·m of starting torque, and gives no rumble figure.137

How should I clean records?#

Start with hands. The Library of Congress says to handle discs by the edge and label, with clean, dry hands, and to let discs from cool storage reach room temperature before playing.138 It also says to brush the stylus with a dense bristle brush before each side.133

Then the records. People argue about the best method more than about what each one does:

Why does static matter?#

Fresh PVC records come out of the mold with a strong negative charge, which pulls in dust.139 A 1980 microscope study in Audio rated static a minor cause of crackle next to groove fracture; its main harm is attracting grit that scours the groove.141 Remedies in the literature include carbon-fiber brushes, conductive mats, polonium-210 neutralizer bars and anti-static compounds; detergent sprays give only "very temporary relief."139 The Library of Congress recommends high-density polyethylene inner sleeves.138

How should I store records?#

Upright, on edge, with immovable dividers every 4-6 inches, and without mixing sizes.138 Records are heavy: the Library of Congress puts grooved discs at more than 35 pounds per shelf-foot.138 At home it suggests room temperature or below and about 35-40% relative humidity, away from attics and basements; for permanent storage, 46-50°F and 30-40% RH.138 The Library's own advice has shifted: its older 2002 "Nutshell" sheet gave 45-50% RH for long-term storage.133

What warps a record?#

Heat. It relaxes the stresses molded into the PVC, and a hot car is the classic case. Keep records below 70°F (21°C) and out of light; flattening a warp with heat and pressure is "never certain."139 At about 140°F (60°C) warping is all but guaranteed, and in a 2021 Discogs survey, known only secondhand, 22% of collectors named warps as their most common damage.142 Short dividers and mixed diameters on a shelf may contribute to warping too.133

Why does my record keep skipping?#

A skip is the stylus losing the groove. The usual causes: a deck that is not level, tracking force too light, anti-skate wrong (skipping outward means too much), a dirty or worn stylus, a warp, worn grooves (white streaks, skips in the same spot), and dust.130 A record weight can flatten a warp but is not a permanent fix.130 Sometimes the record arrived that way: a 1980 microscope study found small holes and surface flaws even on an unplayed audiophile pressing.141

Does playing a record wear it out?#

Yes, and the two best-known studies disagree about how. In RCA's 1968 electron-microscope study by Woodward and colleagues, a 0.2 by 0.7 mil elliptical at 1.5 g left visible "plastic-flow of the groove wall" after one play, building over 10 and 50 plays; a 0.7 mil conical at 5 g nearly wiped out 20 kHz detail after 50 plays; the same conical at 1.5 g showed essentially no wear after 50.129 The study put a critical tracking weight somewhere between 1.5 g and 5 g, and its authors still preferred the elliptical, since the conical's first-play distortion was more than twice as high.129

In 1980, Walter E. Davies played records at 1 g with anti-skate set, at an estimated 30,000-69,000 psi of contact pressure, and found mostly small chip-like fractures rather than large permanent deformation.141 After 50 plays one record was "almost unlistenable," critical listeners heard loss after 2-10 plays, and the vinyl's formulation strongly affected wear.141 So Woodward saw flow and Davies saw fracture, in different decades with different vinyl and pickups. Where they meet is practical: lighter, correctly set tracking and a clean stylus and record go easier on the groove.

Track 7

Records and DJs

The short version: DJs turned the record player into an instrument. A Japanese direct-drive turntable launched in 1972 became the club standard, scratching came out of the Bronx in the mid-1970s (exactly when, and who was first, is still argued), and since the early 2000s timecode vinyl has let DJs keep a record under their hand while playing digital files.

How did a hi-fi turntable end up in every club?#

Matsushita, now Panasonic, launched the Technics SL-1200 in October 1972, developed the year before by a team led by Shuichi Obata.143 It used direct drive, with the platter sitting on the motor (drive types are covered in playing records). For DJs that meant high torque, a near-instant start and steady speed with less wow and flutter, and a platter that could be pushed around by hand or spun backward without harm.135 Belt drives were less consistent and prone to failure, so in the belt era DJs developed the slip cue (hold the record still while the platter turns, then let go), which works better on direct drive anyway. As IEEE Spectrum puts it, "Scratching was impossible on belt drives, because it would destroy them."135

Technics says that in August 1973 DJ Kool Herc used "a pair of Technics direct drive turntables" in the Bronx for his breakbeat technique - a maker's story, and it doesn't name the model.136 The 1979 SL-1200MK2 is billed by Technics as "the first turntable specifically developed for use in discos,"136 and its main change was braking that matched its acceleration.143 As the club scene grew through the 1980s, the series became the de facto standard turntable in clubs.136 An SL-1210MK2 is on display at London's Science Museum.143 IEEE Spectrum calls the direct-drive turntable "the only music-playback device to become a musical instrument itself."135

How many were sold?#

Nobody independent has counted. Technics says 3.5 million units "over the past 35 years," without saying when those 35 years ended.136 The other figure in circulation, "more than 3 million units sold," traces back to Technics too.143 Both are the maker's own cumulative totals, not audited sales.

When did it stop, and when did it come back?#

Panasonic announced the end of Technics analog turntables on November 1, 2010, through the DMC World DJ Championships home page,143 and IEEE Spectrum counts 38 years of production ending that year.135 At CES 2016 the deck returned as the SL-1200G and a limited SL-1200GAE of 1,200 units; the GAE shipped in April 2016 and the G in October, at a reported launch price of about USD 4,000 / GBP 3,100 / EUR 3,499.143 The SL-1200MK7 followed at CES 2019,143 which Technics calls "a full revival of the feel of the SL-1200MK2."136 Production is said to have moved from Japan to Malaysia in 2021, though the evidence for that is thin.143

Who invented scratching, and when?#

The usual story: Grand Wizzard Theodore (Theodore Livingston), about 12 years old in the Bronx, found it by accident, moving a record back and forth while his mother told him to turn the music down.144 KEXP dates that discovery to 1975 and his public debut to 1977 at the Sparkle club, with Grandmaster Flash and Theodore's older brother, Mean Gene, as mentors.144 KEXP is careful to add that it is "difficult to attribute specific people or dates": he "might not be the first person" to rock a record back and forth, only the first known to see what it could do.144

Push on the date and it slides around. The Vinyl Factory puts the Theodore story in the "early 1980s" and calls it legend.145 IEEE Spectrum has club DJs scratching in the "early 1970s."135

Is turntablism older than hip-hop?#

Records as instruments go back further. John Cage's Imaginary Landscape No. 1 (1939) used records switched between speeds, Pierre Schaeffer's Etude aux Chemins de Fer (1948) was built from manipulated recordings of steam locomotives, and Cage's 33 1/3 (1969) was written for 12 record players.145 DMC has run its annual World DJ Championships since 1985, and in 1995 DJ Babu of the Beat Junkies coined the word turntablism.145 Technics dates the concept of turntablism to the 1990s.136

How does timecode vinyl work?#

A DVS (digital vinyl system) swaps the music on the record for a control signal. The turntable's output goes into an audio interface or mixer, software reads the signal for speed, direction and position, plays a digital file to match, and sends that audio back to the mixer like any other deck.146 There is always some latency, and lower is better.146

Open-source developers on the Mixxx project have taken the signals apart. Serato's control record carries a 1,000 Hz tone with the right channel shifted a quarter cycle (90 degrees) against the left; comparing the two at each zero crossing tells forward from backward, and counting crossings, 2,000 a second at normal speed, gives speed.147 In relative mode the software uses only speed and direction; in absolute mode it also reads where the needle is, so dropping the needle further in jumps the track.147 Position comes from bits riding on the carrier: Serato uses amplitude modulation and a 20-bit sequence with up to 1,048,575 states, while Traktor's MK2 timecode uses a 2,500 Hz carrier, about 2.5 times Serato's resolution, and a 110-bit sequence.148 These are reverse-engineered findings, not vendor specs.

Where did DVS come from?#

Chris Bauer's Spacedeck project dates to 1998, and Final Scratch, released in 2001, was "the first DVS software sold publicly."146 Serato began in 1997, when Steve West was at the University of Auckland in New Zealand and wrote a pitch and tempo algorithm, and Serato Scratch Live came out with Rane in 2004.149 Native Instruments' Traktor Scratch reached stores worldwide in May 2007 at a suggested USD 669 / EUR 599, with a cheaper crossgrade for owners of FinalScratch and Serato Scratch Live.150 In 2011 the DMC championships began allowing vinyl-emulation systems.145 For how turntables, CDJs and DVS rigs sit together in a working club booth, see https://showfile.cold.fyi/.

Why do DJs still buy records?#

No one has published a survey of why working DJs still buy vinyl, so the answer stays short. Direct-drive decks were built to be handled, pushed and reversed,135 DVS exists to keep that hand-on-platter control while the music comes from files,146 and Technics judged demand strong enough to restart production in 2016 and add the MK7 in 2019.143

Published sales data is scarce and mostly about hardware. Citing MI Salestrak figures, DJ TechTools reported that turntables took about 14% of US unit sales of DJ technology in May 2016, against 6.3% for CDJs and 42% for controllers.151 On the record side (see what a record costs), Resident Advisor reported in 2023 that pressing costs and plant delays had labels and shops asking whether the dance 12-inch was on the way out; one label cofounder said pressing 300 records had gone from little more than GBP 1,000 to close to GBP 2,000, and 12-inch singles from about GBP 9 to GBP 14.152

Collecting and grading records

The short version: Collectors chase first pressings, but the codes in the dead wax only get you part of the way, and collectors can't even agree on what counts as a "second pressing".100 Condition is graded on a shared ladder from Mint to Poor: the Goldmine scale in the US and on Discogs, the Record Collector scale in the UK.153154 Discogs users added 114.2 million items to their collections in 2025, a company record, and Record Store Day has run every year since 2008.155156

What makes a first pressing a first pressing?#

A first pressing is a copy from the first production run, and matrix data alone won't prove you have one: cut 1 may have been rejected, and reissues often restart at 1.100 The term stretches, too. Collectors may call any change of track list, running order, label design, typo or cover a "second pressing".100

So collectors stack evidence. One collector's routine starts with Discogs and its photos, then dates the label design, reads the dead wax, measures the pressing ring on the label, and cross-checks specialist forums, because Discogs has errors.110 A shop guide adds the jacket: tip-on construction, inserts, copyright years, label addresses, and the barcode test, since barcodes were not widely used before the late 1970s.109 Reading the runout covers the codes themselves, including why Columbia's 1A to 1F were all first cuttings.101

The most collected records are not old. In Discogs' 2025 figures, the most-collected releases included a 2015 180 gram reissue of Nirvana's Nevermind and a 2017 picture disc of Taylor Swift's Reputation, and none of the top releases predate 2012.155 Plenty of people want the record, not the first one.

How does record grading work?#

The common US yardstick is the Goldmine standard.154 Top to bottom:153

Grade What it means
Mint (M) "Absolutely perfect in every way - certainly never played, possibly even still sealed"; to be used sparingly, if at all
Near Mint (NM or M-) "A nearly perfect record"; no obvious wear; jacket without creases, folds or seam splits
Very Good Plus (VG+) Signs of careful play; "Record surfaces may show some slight signs of wear"; slight warps that do not affect the sound are acceptable

Goldmine's own guide says Mint "should never be used as a grade unless more than one person agrees" and that Near Mint records are "shiny, with no visible defects."157 VG+ is a record that, "except for a couple minor things, this would be Near Mint," and a VG record "has surface noise, and some scratches may be audible."157 On price, it puts VG at no more than 25% of Near Mint, Good, Good Plus and VG- at 10 to 15%, and Poor and Fair at 0 to 5%, and it notes that most records are graded visually.157

Two things trip up new buyers. Mint should be rare on any honest listing. And the in-between grades on listings are not all official. Glide Magazine calls NM "a true Goldmine grade" and says M-, VG++ and VG- are not,158 though Goldmine's current guide lists VG- right alongside Good and Good Plus.157

Grading is done by eye and by ear. A record can look clean and still crackle, which is why the VG definition is written in terms of what you hear in quiet passages.153

Which grading scale: Goldmine or Record Collector?#

There are two main scales, and they mostly line up. The Record Collector scale is the UK standard; Goldmine is the one most US sellers and Discogs use.154 The visible difference is a grade called Excellent, which sits at roughly Goldmine VG+.154 If you buy from another country, ask which scale the seller means.

What is a grade worth?#

There is no official conversion, only rules of thumb. Glide Magazine's version: a VG+ copy is worth about 50% of the Near Mint price and a VG copy about 25%, with price guides using NM as the base.158 That is seller's shorthand, not law; rarity, demand and the jacket move prices too.

What is Record Store Day?#

Record Store Day grew out of a 2007 gathering of US record-shop owners, and the UK organizers date the first official day to April 19, 2008; the UK joined that same year, brought over by Spencer Hickman, then at Rough Trade.156 Other accounts give either 2007 or 2008 as the founding year.169 The founders were Eric Levin, Michael Kurtz, Carrie Colliton, Amy Dorfman, Brian Poehner and Don Van Cleave, and Metallica launched the first event at Rasputin Music in Mountain View, California.169

Both sides have numbers. Why are there so many versions of the same album?#

A variant is the same album in a different color, cover or retailer-exclusive edition. Taylor Swift's The Life of a Showgirl, released October 3, 2025, sold 1.2 million vinyl copies on its first day, beating the previous single-week vinyl record of 859,000 set by The Tortured Poets Department.181 Its first-week report listed Target CDs, a Target vinyl, several vinyl "collections", a standard LP and a cassette.181 By March 2026 Luminate counted 27 physical editions.31

The same report counted 4.002 million equivalent album units (sales plus streams and track downloads) and 3,479,500 pure album sales (purchases only) in week one.181

Whether a run of editions rewards superfans or squeezes them is argued both ways. The average number of variants among Billboard 200 top-10 albums went from 3.3 a week in 2019 to 8.9 a week in 2023.182 Industry executives describe variants as a way for fans to show loyalty, support a favorite shop, or find out-of-print catalog again.182 On the other side, in a 2024 Northeastern University piece, music professor Andrew Mall called staggered preorders that make fans pay shipping several times "a wasteful aspect," while also arguing that records are mostly kept rather than thrown away.183

How big is vinyl now?#

In the US, the RIAA's 2025 year-end report put recorded-music revenue at a record $11.5 billion, with vinyl revenue passing $1 billion for the first time since 1983, up 9.3%, the 19th straight year of growth.30 Vinyl was 75% of US physical revenue; CDs brought in $312 million, down nearly 8%.184 In the first half of 2026 the RIAA reported about $6 billion in total revenue, physical up 25.9%, vinyl up 17.7%, and CDs up 58.6%.32

Who bought them: The Life of a Showgirl sold 1.6 million US vinyl copies in 2025, over 3% of all vinyl units; Sabrina Carpenter's Man's Best Friend sold 292,000 and Kendrick Lamar's GNX 279,000.185 Roughly half of vinyl buyers were nonwhite, and about two in five records were sold at independent stores.185

How big is vinyl outside the US?#

The UK bought about 7.6 million vinyl LPs in 2025, up 13.3% after 9.1% growth in 2024, its 18th straight year of growth; streaming was still 89.3% of album consumption.29 The Life of a Showgirl topped UK vinyl too, with over 147,000 copies, the most for any release in a calendar year since the Official Charts Company launched in 1994, and 126,000 of them in its first week.29186 Germany's numbers, and where all these records are pressed, are in where records are made.

Vinyl and the planet

The short version: Most records are pressed from PVC (polyvinyl chloride), a petroleum-based plastic. Kyle Devine of the University of Oslo estimates the US recording industry used far less plastic in 2016 than at the vinyl peak of 1977, but produced more greenhouse gas, because storing and streaming digital files takes energy too. Makers are trying bio-based PVC, bioplastics and injection molding; the gains they report are their own claims, not independent measurements.

What is a record made of, and how is it pressed?#

A conventional LP starts as a lump of PVC called a biscuit (or puck), placed between two stampers in a steam-heated press and squeezed flat (how pressing works).187 On a manual press one cycle takes about 25 seconds.187 Heat, then cooling, then the next one.

The plastic itself is a commodity with a price that moves. One plant owner said in 2022 that the price of PVC had gone up three times since his plant opened in 2020.177 That lands on the cost of every record, and it is part of why some plants have looked at other materials.

Is vinyl worse for the planet than streaming?#

It depends what you count. The best-known numbers cover the whole US recording industry, not any single record. Kyle Devine of the University of Oslo estimated its plastic use and greenhouse gas emissions at four points:179

Year Plastic used (US recording industry) Greenhouse gas
1977 58 million kg 140 million kg
1988 56 million kg 136 million kg
2000 61 million kg 157 million kg
2016 about 8 million kg an estimated 200 to over 350 million kg

Academic estimate (Kyle Devine), published 2019.179

Plastic fell by more than 85% between 1977 and 2016, while estimated emissions rose, with the 2016 figure attributed to storing and transmitting digital files.179

Physical formats were never the whole footprint, and streaming isn't weightless. But 2016 is one year in a format cycle that keeps moving, and industry totals can't tell you what one LP costs the planet against a month of streaming.

The study had an economic half, by Matt Brennan: recorded music fell from about 4.83% of an average US weekly salary in 1977 to about 1.22% in 2013.179

The industry's first carbon footprint report, published in June 2024 by the Vinyl Record Manufacturers Association and the Vinyl Alliance, put a record at about 1.15 kg CO2e on average, measured cradle to factory gate at one pressing plant over 2022-2023.188 For 12-inch LPs it ranged from about 1.07 kg at 140 grams to about 1.28 kg at 200 grams, with PVC compound about half the total and factory energy about 30%.188 The report calls the figure partial and leaves out distribution, use and disposal; it is the industry measuring itself, not an independent audit.188

What is BioVinyl?#

BioVinyl is still PVC, made differently. optimal media in Germany189 launched it with PlastChem in July 2023: the petroleum feedstock in the PVC is replaced with recycled used cooking oil or industrial waste gases.190 That makes it bio-attributed (or bio-based) PVC, and the supply chain is certified under ISCC PLUS, an independent certification standard.190

optimal media says the records are "acoustically and optically identical" to conventional vinyl, come in black plus 11 colors, and carry a "significantly reduced" CO2 footprint - though it puts no number on the reduction.190

Can a record be made without PVC at all?#

Two companies say yes, by different routes.

Evolution Music, a UK company led by CEO Marc Carey, presses records from a bioplastic whose recipe includes sugars and starches, not PVC.191 The company said the records would cost about the same as vinyl at full production.191 On sound, one producer put it at "Maybe 95%" of vinyl191 - a listener's guess, not a measurement.

Green Vinyl Records, in the Eindhoven region of the Netherlands, came out of the CD and DVD industry as a project of the Symcon Group.192 Instead of steam presses, it uses injection molding, forcing molten material into a mold, with its own non-PVC compounds, and prints directly on the disc instead of using paper labels.192 It claims 93% less energy than conventional steam pressing, by its own measurement.192

Do these records sound as good?#

That is the contested part. optimal media says BioVinyl is acoustically identical;190 one producer gave Evolution Music's bioplastic "Maybe 95%."191 Nobody has published an independent listening test or lab comparison, so the verdict is down to your ears.

What about shipping, jackets and shrinkwrap?#

A record also travels: plant to distributor to shop to home, in board, paper and plastic film. The 2024 industry report counts packaging inside its 1.15 kg figure: print packaging (jackets, inserts, sleeves) was about 13% of the footprint and transport packaging about 3%.188 A printed single jacket came to about 0.10-0.15 kg CO2e and a gatefold to 0.20-0.30 kg, while shrinkwrap was about 8 grams CO2e.188 Freight sits outside the figure, and the report's own examples show why it matters: Prague to Melbourne adds about 0.1 kg a record by sea and about 3.46 kg by air, and the report calls international air freight "the elephant in the room."188

What can a buyer or an artist do?#

With one industry estimate per record and no independent ones, the list is short. Artists pressing a run can ask a plant which materials and certifications it offers and what evidence sits behind any environmental claim. Buyers can weigh how often they will play a record against how many editions of it they are tempted to own. The pricing side of those choices is in what a record costs.

Track 11

Where records are made

The short version: The world's biggest presser sits in a small town near Prague: GZ Media, which pressed more than 55 million records in 2021 and runs plants on two continents. North America went from about 15 plants in 2014 to a projected 43 in the US alone by 2022, while Japan still had at least one fully functioning plant in 2010 and Brazil had a second plant on the way in 2016.

Where is the biggest pressing plant in the world?#

In Loděnice, a small town near Prague, where the state-run Gramofonové závody pressed its first record in 1951.88 Today it is GZ Media, which Radio Prague International called the world's biggest vinyl producer in 2022.88

In the mid-1990s the plant made about 300,000 records a year.88 In 2021 it made more than 55 million, and by 2022 it was turning out almost 200,000 a day.88 That is, in one day, two-thirds of what it once made in a year.

GZ Media had grown to eight plants by 2022, including three in North America (Burlington, Ontario; Memphis; Nashville) and one in France (reported, 2022).88

How many plants are there in North America?#

Far more than a decade ago. North America had about 15 plants in 2014; the Vinyl Record Manufacturers Association projected 43 US plants by summer 2022, plus nine in Canada and three in Mexico.177 Those are trade-body estimates, made in 2022 in the middle of a backlog, when lead times "routinely" ran 9-12 months.177

A few US plants publish their prices. National Audio Company in Springfield, Missouri,174 posts a full 2026 price card;173 Gotta Groove Records in Cleveland posted its 180 gram card in 2024.176 What those cards say about the cost of a run is in what a record costs.

Mexico's plants include Vantiva's in Guadalajara, which the company describes as a 273,000-square-foot facility with a future capacity of more than 17 million units a year on 40 presses.193 That capacity is Vantiva's own projection.

Is there still a pressing plant in Japan?#

Yes, at least one, and for a stretch it was described as the only one in Asia. In the late 2000s Time Out Tokyo called Toyo Kasei's Suehiro factory in Yokohama "the only active record pressing plant in Asia."194 In 2010 the Japan Times called Toyo Kasei the owner of "Japan's last fully functioning pressing plant."195 It handles cutting, pressing and label printing in-house, so a record can go from cut to labeled under one roof.194

Those descriptions are from 2010 and the late 2000s, and more presses have come since. In June 2017 Sony Music Entertainment (Japan) said it would resume vinyl production for the first time since 1989, with a press installed at a factory in central Japan; the Japan Times then described Toyo Kasei as "believed to be Japan's only remaining record maker."196 Japan made 799,000 records in 2016, up from 105,000 in 2010, by the Recording Industry Association of Japan's count.196 The label P-Vine finished its own plant, Vinyl Goes Around Pressing, in 2024, and by 2025 Brutus magazine was listing Toyo Kasei and Sony as the other Japanese pressing factories.197 Nobody has published a current count of Japan's plants.

How big is vinyl in the UK?#

The UK bought about 7.6 million vinyl LPs in 2025, up 13.3%, the 18th straight year of growth.29 Its top vinyl album of the year, Taylor Swift's The Life of a Showgirl, sold over 147,000 vinyl copies, the most for any release in a calendar year since the Official Charts Company launched in 1994.29

On the making side, the UK is home to Evolution Music, which presses records from a sugar-and-starch bioplastic rather than PVC.191

Are records made in Germany?#

Germany is where optimal media189 launched BioVinyl with PlastChem in July 2023, a PVC whose petroleum feedstock is replaced with recycled used cooking oil or industrial waste gases.190 The details are in vinyl and the planet.

As a market, Germany leans physical more than most. German music retail turned over EUR 2.42 billion in 2025, up 2.3%; physical formats were 14.2% of revenue; vinyl grew 2.8%; and CDs were still 44.2% of the physical market.198 The industry body BVMI gives no separate euro figure for vinyl.

Are records made in the Netherlands?#

Green Vinyl Records, in the Eindhoven region, came out of the CD and DVD world as a Symcon Group project and injection-molds records from its own non-PVC compounds, printing straight onto the disc instead of using paper labels.192 It claims 93% less energy than steam pressing, by its own measurement.192

Who presses records in Latin America?#

In 2016, Brazil's Polysom in Rio de Janeiro was described as Latin America's only pressing plant, at about 40,000 records a month.199 That January, The Vinyl Factory reported a second Brazilian plant on the way: Vinyl Brazil, in São Paulo on the former Continental Records site, founded by Michel Nath and set to open at about 140,000 records a month.199 That number was a projection, not confirmed output.199

By 2022 the VRMA counted three plants in Mexico.177

Argentina was set to get its first new pressing plant in 2016: Laser Disc, due to start manufacturing at the end of March with two presses, aiming for 40,000 records a month once in full swing.200 That too was a plan, not confirmed output.200
