# The Groove: how a vinyl record gets made

*A field guide to the vinyl record: how the sound gets cut into a lacquer, plated into metal and pressed into plastic, what all those sizes, speeds and colors change, what the scratched codes in the dead wax mean, how a turntable reads a groove, how DJs and collectors use records, what one costs and who gets paid, and where in the world they are made. Written for anyone, anywhere.*

A record is a sound you can see. Hold one to the light and the loud passages look wider than the quiet ones, the gaps between songs are smooth bands, and the last half inch before the label carries handwriting: a catalog number, a letter, sometimes a joke, scratched there by the person who cut it.

It is also a format that should not have survived. In the US in 2025, vinyl brought in $1,042.9 million on 46.8 million units, against 29.5 million CDs, in what the RIAA counted as the 19th year of vinyl growth in a row.[^his-riaa-ye2025] In the UK, the BPI counted 7.6 million LPs sold in 2025, an 18th straight rise.[^his-bpi-2025]

This guide explains how all of it works, start to finish: from the [lathe](/cutting/) that cuts the master, through the father, mother and stamper, to the [steam press](/pressing/) and the paper sleeve; then the [needle](/playing/), the [DJ booth](/dj/), the [collector's grading sheet](/collecting/) and the [bill](/money/). It starts with a [short history](/history/), and it comes with a [decoder](/decoder/) for the [runout groove](/runout/), so you can type in what is scratched in your own records and find out who made them.

Every factual claim is footnoted to the manufacturer, standard, filing, archive or reporting it came from, and every price and sales figure carries its year. Sentences without a footnote are our own explanation. Where a fact could not be pinned down, it is listed in the open questions at the end rather than guessed at. This guide is independent and not affiliated with any label, plant, store or brand named in it; cutting engineers, plant crews and collectors are welcome at the corrections desk.
